Question 1: How does the waterfall methodology differ from agile?

Testing: 

* Waterfall: Testing will happen after building.
* Agile: Testing is on going during the coding process

Change: 

* Waterfall: Changes dont happen once a phase is completed.
* Agile: Changes are happening all the time. 

Customer Involvement: 

* Waterfall: Customers will give feedback during the early stages
* Agile: Customers are incorporated during the whole process.

Delivery: 

* Waterfall: Product will be given at the very end.
* Agile: Delivery happens more frequently as the product is much smaller.

Documentation: 

* Waterfall: Focuses on the documentation being done upfront.
* Agile: Leans towards working software over documents.

Structure: 

* Waterfall: Follows a hierarchial process, from planning to building then testing.
* Agile: Focuses on repeating the steps over time.

Question 4: Why requirements change so much
Requirements change frequently because:

Market Changes: Business needs and market conditions evolve rapidly, especially in technology.

Better Understanding: As customers see working versions, they better understand what they really need versus what they initially asked for.

Technical Discoveries: During development, teams may find better technical solutions or limitations that require requirement adjustments.

Stakeholder Feedback: Different stakeholders may have conflicting or evolving visions for the product.

Competitive Landscape: New competitors or technologies may emerge during development, necessitating changes.

Budget/Time Constraints: Initial requirements may need adjustment when reality of implementation costs/time becomes clear.

Regulatory Changes: Especially in industries like finance or healthcare, legal requirements may change during development.

Question 5: Why testing first helps understand requirements
Writing tests first (test-driven development) helps programmers better understand system requirements because:

Forces Deep Understanding: To write a test, you must fully understand what the feature should do, including edge cases.

Clarifies Specifications: The process of writing tests often reveals ambiguities or missing details in requirements.

Concrete Examples: Tests provide executable examples of how the feature should behave, making abstract requirements more tangible.

Focuses on Outcomes: Tests force thinking about the end result (what) rather than implementation details (how).

Identifies Assumptions: Writing tests exposes assumptions the programmer might have made about the requirements.

Provides Immediate Feedback: Tests give quick verification that the implementation matches requirements.

Creates Living Documentation: The test suite becomes an always-up-to-date specification of the system's expected behavior.
